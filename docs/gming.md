# GMing

## The Three Rules

The following rules are essential to the game.  They are ordered here by importance, and they come before all the other rules in this system.

### Rule #1

**If everyone is having a good time, you're doing it right.**

And its corollary: **If someone is having a bad time, you're doing it wrong.**

Table-top RPGs are an unusual game. If you play for the experience (which I do), there is no win state other than having a good experience.  Your character might die. The entire party might die to the big bad villain who then engulfs the world in fire and darkness. But if you had fun along the way, that's a "win".  (Of course, facing tough challenges and victoriously overcoming them with the power of teamwork can be an even stronger win.)

So the goal of the game is for everyone to have a good time. There are a couple nuanced points to this.

First, different players derive enjoyment from different aspects of the game. Some enjoy mastering the rules or playing optimally. Some prefer exploring the fictional world, while others live for the thrill of combat. Some are just there for the social experience of playing a game together. And all players includes the GM here: They need to be having a good time too.

Second, a _good time_ is not the same as "fun all the time". There may be periods of the game where you are not experiencing your preferred aspect of the game. Even when you are, there's a lot of turn-taking and sharing the spotlight. So you're often bouncing back to actively participating and passively letting other have their good time. Like a good book or movie, you might experience a variety of emotions beyond simple "fun". If you're invested in the game, you might experience tension, poignant sadness, etc.

So there are lots of different positive and neutral states for different people during the game, but the corollary of the rule highlight when it has been broken: When someone starting having a bad time. This is moderate to intense negative experience, like feeling angry, frustrated, cheated, threated, marginalized, etc. You might feel those things vicariously as a character and still have a good time; but when you feel them for yourself as a person and a player, that's a bad time.

If this should happen, there is nothing in the game rules to fix it. The solution is of the group to talk to each other.

Some things can help with this communication.  A session zero or pre-game documentation can help to set expectations about what content the game will include and any ground rules for player conduct. If someone is feeling overwhelmed as a player, they should call for a break. They can then pull the GM aside to explain what is causing them to have a bad time, and the GM can help. That might involve adjusting the content of the game or talking ot other players.

The point of Rule #1 is that there is an implicit social contract that affects all players while playing an RPG. Even if more specific details have not been spelled out, the essence is "Don't be a jerk." For example, if you're derailing the party's plans or attacking, robbing, or harassing another character because "it's what my character would do", then change you're character. You're breaking Rule #1, the most important rule of the whole game. (The only exception to this would be if there is an established level of high trust and communication between all players, and they really would prefer to play a game where your character is a jerk over a character where they are not.)

This rule tends to enforce itself naturally given enough time. If the GM is not having a good time, they will burnout and stop running the game. If a player is having a bad time, they will stop playing. Since a bad time will end your game and probably have negative consequence beyond your game, it's paramount to avoid or address such events early.

### Rule #2

**The GM is the final arbitrator.**

The Game Master is call that for a reason: They are the Master of the game. They typically create and present the world, play the NPCs, guide the story, and adjudicate the rules. When they are doing this much lifting, there is no game without them. For this reason, what they say goes.

However, this is the second rule, not the first. It is in service of Rule #1 that a good GM should strive to be fair to all players, to make consistent and impartial rulings, and to aim for everyone to have a good time.

GM's are human, though. They get tired or distracted. They make mistakes. Help your GM out whenever you can. If they're being inconsistent, you talk about it together. If that discussion starts to drag out and derail the game, then table it and go with the GM's call for now. You can resume the discussion after the current session.

However, if the GM is consistently making calls that cause someone to have a bad time, then they're breaking Rule #1. This means the group has a decision to make.  Either the GM needs to adjust or compromise, or the group's composition needs to change.

### Rule #3

**The rules provide a shared foundation for collaborative play.**

The rules establish an expectation about what the player's characters can do in the world, and how likely they are to succeed at certain tasks.  By learning the rules, they can make informed and strategic choices for their characters.

There is nothing sacred about this particular set of rules when it comes to serving that goal. The GM can tweak or change them to reflect the needs and desires of their group. But, to maintain a shared foundation, those changes should be clearly communicated. Building out a set of precedents or table rules is an example of doing this.

## Presenting the World

don't be afraid to share stats: DCs, ACs, HD, etc.

* whether a chasm is 8 or 10 feet wide is less useful to a player than whether it is DC10 or DC15 to leap across it.
* A DC or AC can concretely describe what a character in that world would be able to deduce.  When see a knight in plate mail or a giant beetle with thick carapace, those characters should have a pretty good idea that this character has a high AC.  If huge wolf that stands shoulder high is slowly circling, heavy muscles rippling beneath it's fur, putting a number like 3 HD or 5 HD is pretty good way to communicate just how scary or powerful this dire wolf looks.
* If a stat would not be obvious at first sight, then by all means don't reveal it. However, you can reveal it as soon as it does become obvious. For example, you take a swing at the unarmored fey gentleman, but he easily leans back and avoids your blow. Another swing and he stumbles back. It looks like his AC is 15.

### Movement Modes (Athletics)

Most short athletic tasks that involve movement--such as climbing, leaping, running, or swimming--should offer a choice between using STR or DEX.  While high STR is helpful here, high STR also typically means that your own body is heavier. A halfling or child, with less body mass to move, may be just as capable at athletics using DEX as a heavy warrior who relies on STR.
